## ANONYMISED CASE STUDIES (SCS Youth Case Management Training Reference)

All names, handles, dates, and identifying details have been changed.
These case studies are used for staff training and AI-assisted guidance only.

=== CASE STUDY 001: Early Identification Prevents Escalation ===

Category: Self-Harm Ideation | Initial Risk Score: 3 | Final Score: 2 (de-escalated)

Background:
A 16-year-old with no prior contact with SCS was flagged by the AI system for a combination of late-night posting activity and increasing use of emotionally charged vocabulary. The content did not explicitly reference self-harm, but sentiment analysis detected a sustained downward shift over 10 days.

Helper Response:
The assigned helper, noticing the low-pressure nature of the signals, decided to send Template A (General Warm Check-In) rather than a more urgent contact. The youth responded within 6 hours expressing surprise that "someone noticed."

What worked:
- Non-alarmist initial contact created psychological safety.
- The helper followed the low-risk protocol precisely: no mention of the AI detection.
- Three follow-up messages over two weeks, all brief and warm.
- Referral to CHAT (Community Health Assessment Team) was accepted on the second conversation.

Outcome:
Youth engaged in counselling voluntarily. Case closed with no escalation. Risk score dropped to 2 over 3 ingestion cycles.

Lessons:
- Early detection at risk 3 is preventive: waiting for score 4–5 increases intervention difficulty.
- Response to initial contact is not guaranteed; do not over-read silence in the first 24h.
- Document everything — even "no response" logs are valuable for the audit trail.

=== CASE STUDY 002: Coordinated Cyberbullying with Doxxing ===

Category: Cyberbullying | Initial Risk Score: 4 | Escalation: Yes

Background:
A 14-year-old female was identified as the target of a coordinated harassment campaign from multiple accounts. Partial personal information (school name, neighbourhood) was shared in comment threads. The youth had completely stopped posting for 5 days.

Helper Response:
The helper immediately flagged for escalation before outreach, as per cyberbullying protocol. Platform reports were filed for all identified accounts. The school liaison officer was contacted after senior approval.

What worked:
- Helper prioritised safety documentation before reaching out.
- Outreach used Template F with personalised additions acknowledging that things "hadn't been easy online lately."
- Two-meeting school coordination led to a supportive meeting with a school counsellor.

What was challenging:
- The youth initially declined contact ("I don't want to talk about it").
- Helper correctly respected this boundary, re-checked risk score at next ingestion, and sent a brief Follow-Up Template C two weeks later.
- On the second follow-up, youth agreed to a brief conversation.

Outcome:
Perpetrator accounts suspended. Youth returned to posting within 3 weeks. Case moved to followup status. Closed at 8 weeks.

Lessons:
- Respecting a youth's refusal of contact is not abandoning them — it builds trust.
- Never contact perpetrators directly; this almost always escalates the situation.
- Platform reporting should happen regardless of whether the youth is engaged.

=== CASE STUDY 003: Family Conflict Escalation ===

Category: Family Conflict | Initial Risk Score: 3 | Escalated to Risk 5

Background:
A 15-year-old began posting about arguments at home. Initial risk score was 3 with no immediate safety concern. Over the next ingestion cycle (48h), new signals emerged including references to "not being safe" and "having nowhere to go."

Helper Response:
The helper reviewed the escalated signals and immediately contacted the team lead. The decision to treat this as an active safety concern was made before any outreach.

What worked:
- Helper used Template H but added: "I want to make sure you're safe — are you somewhere you feel okay right now?"
- The youth responded with an affirmative but indicated the home situation was worsening.
- Over 3 conversations, the helper built enough trust to facilitate a referral to a Family Service Centre with the youth's consent.

Critical decision point:
- At risk 5 (youth indicated feeling unsafe at home), the helper correctly escalated to senior case manager, who initiated the FSC referral process.
- No contact was made with the family without the youth's explicit consent.

Outcome:
Family mediation through the FSC led to improvement in home situation. Youth's risk score reduced to 2 over 4 ingestion cycles. Ongoing monitoring.

Lessons:
- Risk scores can change rapidly between ingestion cycles; treat each new signal set with fresh assessment.
- Never rush to contact family — this can escalate harm if the conflict source is within the family.
- Document every conversation and decision, especially safety assessments.

=== CASE STUDY 004: Persistent Isolation with Masked Distress ===

Category: Loneliness / Isolation | Initial Risk Score: 2 | Escalated to Risk 4

Background:
A 16-year-old showed gradual disengagement signals over 6 weeks. The initial score of 2 did not trigger urgent action. At week 4, frequency metrics showed zero peer interactions and a dramatic shift to passive consumption.

Helper Observation:
A helper reviewing the case at the next ingestion noticed that the language in the youth's remaining posts had shifted — moving from neutral to increasingly hopeless-sounding: "I guess it doesn't matter" and "whatever."

What worked:
- The helper did not wait for the AI score to escalate; they flagged the case for senior review based on their own clinical observation of the language shift.
- Outreach using Template I was personalised: "I know online spaces can feel empty sometimes, but I wanted you to know someone's here."
- Youth responded after 3 days.

Mistake nearly made:
- Initially, the assigned helper was going to close the case as "low concern." This was appropriately questioned by a senior in a supervision meeting.

Outcome:
Early escalation prevented what could have become a self-harm ideation case. Youth connected to a youth social group. Risk score stabilised at 2.

Lessons:
- AI scores are inputs, not verdicts. A score of 2 combined with specific language patterns warrants human review.
- Use supervision sessions to flag cases you're uncertain about.
- Isolation can be a precursor to more serious categories — monitor carefully.

=== CASE STUDY 005: Academic Stress Co-Occurring with Self-Harm Risk ===

Category: Academic Stress / Self-Harm Ideation | Risk Score: 3 → 5

Background:
A case flagged as Academic Stress at score 3 was initially assigned to monitoring only. Within one ingestion cycle, the vocabulary shifted from exam stress to hopelessness: "what's the point of trying", "it's all useless anyway."

Re-categorisation:
The helper reviewing the case escalated the category to Self-Harm Ideation overlay and changed the priority to critical.

What worked:
- Protocol explicitly states: "academic stress combined with hopeless or worthless language: treat as Self-Harm risk."
- Template E was used with personalisation for the academic context: "I know exams can feel like everything right now, but I want to check in — how are you really doing?"
- Youth opened up about pressure from family expectations alongside academic stress.

Referral:
Youth was referred to a school counsellor (with consent) and a SCS mental health volunteer.

Outcome:
The category re-assignment was the pivotal decision. Early intervention at the crossover point prevented further escalation. Risk score 3 at closure.

Lessons:
- Categories are not siloed. Cross-category risk is common.
- Re-categorise if the language patterns shift, regardless of initial assignment.
- Academic stress can be a surface presentation for deeper distress — always probe gently.

=== CASE STUDY 006: Recurring High-Risk User ===

Category: Self-Harm Ideation | Second Case | Risk Score: 4

Background:
A youth (anonymised as User X) had a previous case closed at low risk 6 months prior. A new ingestion cycle detected re-emergence of distress signals — different account, similar patterns.

Case Considerations:
- The prior case history was visible to the helper via the case timeline.
- The prior case had no escalation; this was a new escalation threshold.

What worked:
- Helper proactively reviewed the history before outreach, understanding that this was a possible relapse pattern.
- Outreach referenced the prior positive engagement: "Hi [Name], we spoke a while back and I've been thinking about you. Wanted to check in again."
- Youth remembered the prior contact and was more receptive.

Outcome:
Faster engagement due to prior relationship. Case managed to risk 2 within 3 ingestion cycles.

Lessons:
- Prior case history is an asset. Review it before outreach on recurring users.
- Do not treat a second case as entirely new — acknowledge the relationship if prior contact was positive.
- Recurring users may have underlying chronic stressors that require longer-term support planning.
