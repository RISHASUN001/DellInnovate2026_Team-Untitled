SCHOOL OF CHILD SAFEGUARDING (SCS) — PROTOCOL DOCUMENT
Title: Substance Use Concern Protocol — Youth
Category: Substance Use
Version: 2.0 | Last Updated: October 2024

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 1: OVERVIEW
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Substance use in youth is a complex issue involving experimentation, peer influence, coping with distress, and in some cases addiction. SCS helpers do not approach substance use punitively but through a harm reduction and support lens.

Substances commonly involved in Singapore youth cases:
- Cannabis (most common, increasingly accessible via online ordering)
- Alcohol (readily available, culturally normalised in some contexts)
- Inhalants (among younger cohorts)
- Vaping / e-cigarettes (legal age in SG is 21)
- Prescription medication misuse (sedatives, stimulants)
- Less commonly: MDMA, methamphetamine (in older youth clubbing contexts)

Legal context: Cannabis possession in Singapore carries mandatory minimum sentences. Helpers must be aware of confidentiality boundaries around disclosures.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 2: DETECTION SIGNALS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Social Media Indicators:
- Cannabis leaf emoji (🍃), "🌿", "420", "lit", "high" in youth context
- Drinking posts with underage peers
- Photos showing drug paraphernalia
- Posts about buying or distributing substances
- References to blacking out, hangovers, or substance-induced behaviour

School/Family Reports:
- Smell of alcohol or cannabis
- Red or glassy eyes
- Unexplained mood changes
- Money missing or unexplained cash
- Change in friend group to older peers
- Academic decline coinciding with behavioural change

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 3: RISK STRATIFICATION
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Level 1 — Experimental (Low Risk): 
Single or infrequent use. Social context only. Youth shows insight into risks. Good protective factors.
→ Psychoeducation. No formal intervention required unless pattern changes.

Level 2 — Regular Use (Moderate Risk): 
Regular use (weekly or more), starting to impact school or relationships. Peer group also using.
→ Outreach contact. Motivational interviewing. Parent notification (with consent if 14+). School welfare officer informed.

Level 3 — Problematic Use (High Risk):
Daily or near-daily use. Withdrawal symptoms evident. Using to cope with distress. Obtaining substances independently.
→ Clinical referral (National Addictions Management Service — NAMS). Parent notification. School absence monitoring.

Level 4 — Dependency/Distribution (Critical):
Signs of physical or psychological dependence. Evidence of distributing or obtaining for others.
→ Refer to NAMS for assessment. If distribution: duty to consider whether SPF report is required (supervisor decision). Youth welfare remains primary concern.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 4: MOTIVATIONAL INTERVIEWING APPROACH
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Do not lecture, threaten, or exaggerate risks — this increases resistance. Use Motivational Interviewing (MI):

OARS framework:
- Open questions: "What do you enjoy about it? What do you not like about it?"
- Affirmations: "I can see you've thought about this."
- Reflective listening: "So you use it to relax after school."
- Summarising: "So on one hand... and on the other hand..."

Explore ambivalence:
- "What are the good things about using? What are the not-so-good things?"
- "Has it ever caused problems for you?"
- "If things kept going the way they are, what would your life look like in 5 years?"
- "On a scale of 1–10, how important is it to you to change this?"

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 5: WORKING WITH PARENTS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Parents often react with panic, anger, or denial. Prepare them:
- This is a health issue, not purely a moral failure.
- Punishment alone rarely works and may drive use underground.
- Open communication and unconditional love are the strongest protective factors.
- Professional help is available (NAMS family services).

Refer parents to:
- NAMS Family Education Programme
- Family Service Centres (FSCs) for counselling
- Narcotics Anonymous Family Groups

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 6: CONFIDENTIALITY AND LEGAL OBLIGATIONS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

SCS helpers maintain confidentiality about substance use disclosures UNLESS:
1. There is risk to the youth's safety (overdose risk, dangerous combinations)
2. There is evidence of supply to other minors
3. The youth requests help to stop

SCS does not report youth to CNB (Central Narcotics Bureau) for personal use without supervisor consultation and legal review.

For serious cases (distribution, trafficking involvement): consult SCS legal counsel before any police engagement.
