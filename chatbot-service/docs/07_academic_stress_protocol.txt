SCHOOL OF CHILD SAFEGUARDING (SCS) — PROTOCOL DOCUMENT
Title: Academic Stress, Perfectionism and School Refusal Intervention
Category: Academic Stress
Version: 2.5 | Last Updated: December 2024

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 1: CONTEXT AND PREVALENCE
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Singapore's high-pressure academic culture creates significant stress among youth. According to the Singapore Examinations and Assessment Board (SEAB), primary and secondary school students face high-stakes examinations at ages 12 and 16. Research consistently shows that exam-related anxiety affects 30–40% of secondary school students in Singapore at clinically concerning levels.

Academic stress becomes a safeguarding concern when it:
- Leads to significant sleep disruption (less than 6 hours/night consistently)
- Results in physical symptoms (headaches, nausea, appetite loss)
- Triggers self-harm or suicidal ideation
- Causes school refusal or truancy
- Is accompanied by parental pressure that constitutes emotional abuse

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 2: DETECTION SIGNALS
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Social Media Indicators:
- Posts expressing extreme exam fear ("I'll die if I fail")
- Sleep deprivation posts (late-night study posts, exhaustion descriptions)
- Perfectionism language ("must get A", "not good enough", "failure")
- Physical complaints (headache, stomach ache, not eating) linked to school
- Comparing self unfavourably to peers academically
- Expressing shame or worthlessness tied to academic performance

School-reported indicators:
- Sudden grade drops after high performance
- Unexplained absences, particularly around exam periods
- Physical complaints leading to early dismissal
- Withdrawal from CCAs previously enjoyed
- Teacher reports of tearfulness, distraction, or uncharacteristic outbursts

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 3: ASSESSMENT — ACADEMIC DISTRESS INDEX (ADI)
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Ask the youth:
1. "On a scale of 1–10, how stressed are you about school right now?"
2. "How many hours of sleep are you getting each night?"
3. "Are you able to eat regular meals?"
4. "When did you last do something enjoyable that wasn't school-related?"
5. "Do your parents put pressure on you about grades?"
6. "Have you ever felt like life isn't worth living because of school?"

High concern indicators: score >7 on stress scale, <6 hours sleep, appetite disruption, positive response to Q6.

───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
SECTION 4: INTERVENTION APPROACH
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

4.1 Cognitive Reframing
Help youth identify and challenge unhelpful thought patterns:
- "If I fail this exam, my life is over" → Challenge: What is actually likely to happen?
- "I'm worthless if I don't get A" → Challenge: Is your worth defined by grades?
- "I must be perfect" → Explore where this belief comes from.

4.2 Practical Skills
- Time management: introduce Pomodoro technique, study schedule templates.
- Sleep hygiene: no devices 1 hour before bed, consistent sleep schedule.
- Stress management: deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation.
- Exercise: even 20 minutes of physical activity reduces cortisol.

4.3 Addressing Parental Pressure
- Engage parents with empathy: acknowledge their hopes for their child.
- Psychoeducation: extreme pressure reduces performance, not increases it.
- Share research: anxiety impairs working memory and exam performance.
- Suggest positive reinforcement strategies.
- If pressure rises to emotional abuse, consider MSF CPS referral.

4.4 School Coordination
- Request teacher to reduce pressure language (e.g., "must score well" → "do your best").
- Explore exemptions or modifications for youth with anxiety (school counsellor can facilitate).
- For school refusal: graduated exposure plan with school counsellor.

SECTION 6: SCHOOL REFUSAL — SPECIFIC PROTOCOL
───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

If youth is refusing to attend school (5+ days unexplained absence):
1. Distinguish between school refusal (anxiety-driven) and truancy (choice-driven).
2. For school refusal: engage compassionately, do not punish.
3. Graduated return plan:
   - Week 1: visit school premises with a support person (no need to attend class)
   - Week 2: attend 1 class with support person available
   - Week 3: attend 3 classes independently
4. Coordinate between SCS helper, school counsellor, and parents.
5. If refusal persists >4 weeks: refer to CGC for clinical assessment.

Legal note: Under the Compulsory Education Act, persistent unexplained absence (10+ school days) requires notification to MOE School Advisory Team.
